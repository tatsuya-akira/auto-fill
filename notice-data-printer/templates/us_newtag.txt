We write to you on behalf of our client Eli Lilly and Company (“Lilly”). As you may be aware, Lilly is one of the world’s largest pharmaceutical companies (see www.lilly.com) and has been in the pharmaceutical industry for over 145 years. During this time, Lilly has consistently been at the forefront of significant medical breakthroughs, including its Mounjaro® and Zepbound® medications for the treatment of type 2 diabetes and obesity in 2022 and 2023 and Foundayo™ for obesity in 2026.  Mounjaro® and Zepbound® are the first and only FDA-approved GIP and GLP-1 dual receptor agonist utilizing the active ingredient tirzepatide.  Foundayo™ is the first and only FDA-approved medicine using the active ingredient orforglipron. 

Because demand for Lilly’s FDA-approved medicines is high, many actors have attempted to mass-manufacture and sell unapproved, compounded versions of tirzepatide or orforglipron.  As FDA has made clear, compounded drugs are not the same as FDA-approved medicines.  They do not undergo clinical testing, are not reviewed and approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for safety and efficacy, and the entities that make them do not have to comply with the same rigorous safety and sterility requirements as manufacturers of FDA-approved products.  This is why FDA has repeatedly cautioned that “[u]nnecessary use of compounded drugs may expose patients to potentially serious health risks.”

Despite the fact that FDA has never reviewed the safety, efficacy, or quality of compounded tirzepatide products, many entities marketing these products wrongfully equate them with Lilly’s FDA-approved medications.  Specifically, certain users of your platform falsely and misleadingly promote compounded products by likening or comparing them to Lilly’s FDA-approved products, claiming they are clinically proven to be effective, and representing or implying that they are FDA-approved or use FDA-approved ingredients.  FDA has made clear that these promotional claims are illegal.  Between September 2025 and March 2026, FDA issued more than 80 warning letters to entities for violations of federal law due to misleading promotion of compounded obesity drugs.  These warning letters cautioned that any claims that state or imply that compounded tirzepatide products “have been FDA-approved or otherwise evaluated for safety and effectiveness” are “false and misleading.”   For example, FDA identified the following statements regarding compounded tirzepatide as “false and misleading”:

•	“from the same family as GLP-1 treatments like Mounjaro® and Zepbound®” 
•	“Tirzepatide (generic Zepbound)” 
•	“Clinically Proven Weight Loss – Tirzepatide has been shown to….” 
•	“With the same active ingredient as Mounjaro® and Zepbound®, this treatment has been clinically tested and FDA-approved for effective weight management.” 
•	“Hit weight management goals with the same weight loss ingredient as brand-name GLP-1 medications, at a fraction of the cost” 
•	“Clinically shown to support 15-22% average body weight reduction.” 
•	“Compounding tirzepatide involves creating small, customized batches of the medication in an FDA-monitored, state-licensed pharmacy, using FDA-approved ingredients….” 
•	“Safe, affordable, and effective.” 

FDA also issued a statement from Commission Martin Makary in February 2026 making clear:  “[C]ompanies cannot claim that non-FDA-approved compounded products are generic versions or the same as drugs approved by FDA.”  
Critically, the same statement also emphasized that companies “also cannot state compounded drugs use the same active ingredient as the FDA-approved drugs or that compounded drugs are clinically proven to produce results for the patient.” 
Additionally, FDA has explained that entities selling compounded products cannot obscure the true manufacturer of compounded products.  Specifically, FDA has issued dozens of warning letters to sellers of compounded products for displaying their brand name “on the pictured label, suggesting [the seller] is the compounder of th[e] drug products when in fact it is not.”   Such representations “are false and misleading” and violate federal law.
